# The governor on an FPGA, in plain English

This folder is where the governor stops being a drawing and becomes a circuit you can hold. Not the final chip, a
stand-in for it that I can buy off the shelf, wire to a Raspberry Pi, and leave running for a week. Here is what the
pieces are, without the jargon, and then everything that went wrong getting here, with the numbers.

## What an FPGA is

A chip made of thousands of tiny logic blocks and a huge web of switchable wires between them. You do not etch it,
you load it: a file tells every block what to be and every switch whether to connect. Load a different file and it
becomes a different circuit.

Everyday analogy: a LEGO baseplate with a big box of bricks. A real chip is a plastic toy out of a mould: cheap by the
million, impossible to change. The FPGA is the LEGO model of the same toy. Slower and more expensive per piece, but I
can build it this afternoon, test it tonight, and rebuild it tomorrow when I find a mistake.

The board I picked is the ULX3S, built around a Lattice ECP5 FPGA with about 84,000 logic blocks. I picked it because
every tool needed to build for it is open source and installs with `pip`, so the whole build runs from one script on
any computer, with nothing to license and nothing to click.

## What SMBus is

SMBus (System Management Bus) is a two-wire party line inside every server: one wire for a clock, one for data. The
management controller uses it to ask parts how hot they are, what they are, whether they are alive. It is I2C with
stricter manners: time limits, and an optional checksum on every message (the PEC, a one-byte fingerprint of the
whole message).

Everyday analogy: the building's intercom. Anyone in the building can press the button and ask the front desk a
question. In this design the governor is a front desk that will answer any question about itself (what state am I
in, which levers are pulled, what is my policy fingerprint) but will take exactly one kind of instruction over the
intercom: "there is a sealed letter in your mailbox." That is the doorbell. Everything that matters arrives in the
letter, signed, and the intercom cannot change anything else. If the message is garbled (bad checksum), cut off, sent
to the wrong desk, or tries to change anything but the doorbell, the desk says "say again" and does nothing.

The part I built is the front desk's ear: `rtl/governor_rtl/smbus.py`. The proof that the intercom cannot change
anything but the doorbell is `rtl/formal/smbus.sv`, and it is checked against every possible pattern on the two
wires, not just the ones I thought of.

## What the bitstream is

The file that tells the FPGA what to become. `hw/fpga/build.sh` makes it in four steps: translate my circuit
description into the standard hardware language (Verilog), turn that into a list of logic blocks (synthesis), decide
where each block sits on the chip and how the wires run (place and route), and pack that into the file the board
loads (the bitstream). The script also checks the result is fast enough: the real chip is planned at 50 MHz, and the
FPGA has to keep up with that, or the tests on it would not mean much.

Everyday analogy: the LEGO instruction booklet, generated by a machine from the drawing, with a check at the end
that the model will not fall over when you pick it up.

## What the bench test is

A Raspberry Pi wired to the FPGA plays two parts. It is the server's management controller on the intercom, asking
questions and sending good and deliberately bad messages. And it stands in for the governor's own firmware, which the
FPGA does not have yet: it delivers heartbeats, clears and halt commands on separate wires, the way the firmware
would after checking a signature. Then the Pi watches the levers (the wires that hold a GPU in reset or brake its
power) and checks they do what the registers say. The long version, the soak, keeps the governor running with a
heartbeat every second for days and counts every time it halts when it should not. That count is the false-halt
rate, the number operators will ask for first.

Everyday analogy: a fire alarm on a test stand. You press the test button, you blow smoke at it, you cut its wire,
and you check it screams every time. Then you leave it alone in a quiet room for a week and count how often it
screams for no reason. Both numbers matter. An alarm that cries wolf gets unplugged.

`hw/fpga/BENCH.md` has the shopping list, the wiring table and the one command.

## What didn't work (every attempt, with the numbers)

Everything below happened while building this, in order. None of it is hidden in a log somewhere; the technical
version with the same numbers is at the end of `REPORT.md`.

1. **The checksum circuit came out enormous.** My first version of the PEC calculator was written in a way that
   copied itself at every step, so the hardware description ballooned to 109,431 lines. Rewritten as the simple
   XOR pattern it really is: 1,451 lines. Same answers, a tiny fraction of the size.

2. **The proof did not go through the first three times.** The prover kept finding situations that look possible on
   paper but cannot happen on a real bus (a counter at 15 when it only counts to 9, for example). Each time I wrote
   down the missing fact and ran it again. Fourth run: proven, in about 30 seconds.

3. **The short proof cannot catch a planted bug by itself.** I plant 11 bugs on purpose (skip the checksum, accept
   writes to read-only registers, answer the wrong address, and so on). The proof catches all 11, but only in its
   second half (induction), because a complete doorbell message takes hundreds of clock cycles and the first half
   only looks 25 cycles deep. So every planted bug is also replayed as real traffic on a simulated bus, where it
   breaks a named rule. 11 of 11 fail there too.

4. **One of my own checks could not fail.** A test meant to prove "the intercom's address wire cannot store
   anything inside the core" passed even after I planted a register on that wire. The tool was quietly throwing
   away its own answer. Fixed; now it finds the planted register and finds nothing in the real core.

5. **The core halts itself the moment it starts (finding RTL-CORE-HALTS-AFTER-REACHING-RUN).** The first simulation of the whole thing with the
   loop sensor running from power-on showed the governor reaching RUN for exactly one clock tick and then halting,
   blaming the safety loop. Its own loop switch was open while it waited to start, so it had already concluded the
   loop was broken and never rechecked. This is a real bug in the existing halt logic, not in the new parts. I did
   not fix it, because that file belongs to other work in flight; there is a test that fails today and a one-line
   proposed fix in `REPORT.md`. Until it is fixed, the bench test will stop at "could not reach RUN", which is the
   right answer.

6. **The first place-and-route never finished.** The default wire router worked for about 20 minutes and was still
   stuck with 38,495 of 88,079 connections fighting over the same wires, not getting better. The other router in the
   same tool finished in 193 seconds.

7. **The FPGA is too slow for 50 MHz (finding RTL-FPGA-MISSES-TARGET-CLOCK).** The real chip is planned at 50 million clock ticks a second. On
   this FPGA the design tops out at about 38 million. The slow part is the capability table, which checks eight
   permission slips and deducts from a budget all in one tick, and most of that time is spent on the FPGA's wiring,
   not its logic. The build then automatically makes a second bitstream at a clock it can keep up with
   (30 MHz, where it has room to spare: it reaches 36.4 MHz in that build). All the governor's timers count real microseconds, so a heartbeat still expires after 5
   seconds and a halt still escalates on schedule; only the electronics underneath run slower.

8. **The cheaper boards are too small, on paper.** The design needs 17,306 logic blocks. The cheaper ULX3S (12F)
   is rated for 12,288. The open tools, though, treat that chip as the bigger 25F chip it is physically made from and
   happily place the design on it (71 percent full). Whether a real 12F board runs it is untested and outside the
   maker's ratings, so I stayed on the 85F. The iCE40 family the brief mentioned tops out at 7,680 blocks, and the
   core alone needs about 14,500, so that was never on.

9. **Two small tool quirks.** The WebAssembly version of the PLL calculator can only save files in the folder it
   runs in, and the WebAssembly version of one Yosys analysis command ran out of memory stack on a design this big.
   Both worked around; neither changes a result.

10. **One price I could not look up.** The Raspberry Pi product page did not load in time, so the shopping list has
    a price only for the ULX3S, where I read one.
